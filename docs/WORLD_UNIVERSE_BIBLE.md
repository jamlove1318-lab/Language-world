# World & Universe Bible — Language-world

**Document status:** Working draft v0.1 — universe framework and recovered Emerald Valley material; world canon is incomplete  
**Purpose:** Establish the shared universe structure and a rigorous standard for designing every world.  
**Reference boundary:** GoPAL-AI is read-only research. The content below is independently organized for Language-world; no old map, scene, renderer, model, pipeline, or implementation is being adopted as a technical dependency.

## 1. The universe in one sentence

Language-world is a connected universe of distinct, living places where language, culture, exploration, stories, mysteries, games, creativity, relationships, and everyday life belong naturally to the world rather than appearing as separate menu activities.

It is not a collection of language-themed levels. It is not one world recoloured for different languages. Each world must have its own identity, geography, atmosphere, history, inhabitants, ecology, sound, activities, and reasons to explore.

## 2. Universe structure

### 2.1 The home world

**Emerald Valley** is the learner's home world: the first place they are welcomed into, learn how the universe works, and can return to over time.

It is not the whole universe, not a container holding every language culture, and not a generic lobby.

### 2.2 Language worlds

Each language has a distinct world or world-region designed around its own geography and lived context. A world may contain real places, fictional places, or carefully blended locations, but the distinction must be clear to the learner.

A language world is not defined only by the language spoken there. It is defined by a coherent place:
- Geography, terrain, climate, waterways, coastlines, mountains, cities, villages, rural areas, and travel distances.
- Architecture, materials, infrastructure, homes, public spaces, workplaces, and gathering places.
- Local history, traditions, contemporary life, arts, foodways, and everyday culture.
- Residents, communities, relationships, occupations, routines, and local stories.
- Wildlife, plants, habitats, weather, seasons, and ecological rhythms.
- Music, ambient sound, acoustic identity, lighting, and visual atmosphere.
- Activities, games, mysteries, adventures, creative opportunities, and learning contexts.

A language can have multiple regions and contrasting environments. Do not compress a whole country or culture into one stereotyped village or one city street.

### 2.3 Other universe spaces

The universe may also contain places that are not ordinary language worlds: shared transit hubs, story realms, special event spaces, dreamlike locations, historical reconstructions, fictional adventure settings, and temporary narrative spaces. Their relationship to the world registry must be explicit; they must not be misrepresented as a language's entire geography.

## 3. Core world principles

1. **A place exists before the learner arrives.** It has a past, present, geography, inhabitants, and activity.
2. **The world is not a UI backdrop.** The learner should be able to understand and explore it without relying on a stack of cards or buttons.
3. **Every place has a reason to exist.** Visual landmarks, sound, residents, objects, stories, and activities should reinforce its identity.
4. **Discovery is not synonymous with collecting.** It can be a view, a sound, a clue, a relationship, a story, a hidden path, a cultural insight, or a changed place.
5. **Learning belongs inside life.** Language practice should arise from meaningful contexts, not be forced into every interaction.
6. **The world is bigger than its learning objectives.** There should be worthwhile experiences even when the learner is not completing a lesson.
7. **The world is bigger than Cassidy.** Cassidy is important, but residents, creatures, places, events, and the learner's own choices also drive the universe.
8. **Continuity matters.** Visits, discoveries, authored changes, and meaningful events should be remembered where the product promises they are.
9. **Absence is not failure.** No world or character should punish the learner for not returning.
10. **Beauty serves identity and readability.** Use 2D, 2.5D, 3D, illustration, sound, and cinematic staging where each is strongest; visual fidelity is not an end in itself.

## 4. Emerald Valley — recovered home-world foundation

### 4.1 Emotional identity

The reference archive describes Emerald Valley as a peaceful, warm, lived-in mountain settlement with a quiet, handcrafted, slightly mysterious, contemplative, rain-friendly and lantern-lit atmosphere. Its central promise is the feeling of arriving somewhere familiar that remembers meaningful visits, while still sensing there is more to discover.

Language-world should retain this emotional direction while developing its own world bible, geography, stories, and art direction.

### 4.2 Geographic structure recovered from the reference

The reference world specification describes an elongated mountain valley:
- **West:** mountain approach and railway entry.
- **Central:** valley floor, stream, paths, café, market, and library.
- **East:** railway tunnel and deeper mountain continuation.
- **North:** mountain wall, elevated terrain, sanctuary, and bamboo garden.
- **South:** lower valley approach and open arrival views.

It identifies Mount Emerald, a Sugi treeline, valley terrain, and the Suzu Stream as important geographic features. The stream is described as a major emotional and geographic spine of the valley.

This is a recovered spatial concept, not an instruction to copy old runtime coordinates or scene geometry.

### 4.3 Landmark and place vocabulary recovered

The reference describes the following connected places:
- **Komorebi Café:** primary social anchor and welcoming everyday gathering place.
- **Café social clearing / campfire:** a secondary gathering place connected to the café.
- **Lantern Market:** warmer, busier social and trading area; Aoi is associated with the market.
- **Whispering Library:** quieter archive/reading space; Yuki is associated with the library.
- **Valley Station and rail crossing:** a travel landmark; Kenji is described as station master.
- **Railway mountain tunnel:** a threshold into deeper geography and possible mystery.
- **Mountain Sanctuary:** elevated place overlooking the valley; Sora is associated with it as a traveller/passing presence.
- **Whisper Bamboo Garden:** quiet natural area.
- **Tea garden / growing area:** Hiro is described as a tea gardener.
- **Mount Emerald, Sugi treeline, Suzu Stream, paths, bridges, clearings, lanterns, and vegetation:** the larger natural framework tying these places together.

These names and roles are useful recovered material. Their final placement, architecture, story importance, resident biographies, and visual design still require a unified Language-world authoring pass.

### 4.4 Atmosphere and environmental identity

The reference world describes distinct environmental responses:
- Morning, afternoon, evening, and midnight each have different lighting and activity.
- Rain darkens terrain, changes water and surfaces, and makes the café/library feel inviting.
- Snow produces a subdued, quieter visual mood.
- Lanterns, the campfire, the stream, and distant train sounds contribute to place identity.
- Ambient sound changes by location: café, market, library, station, stream, garden, and sanctuary.

Language-world should develop a consistent environment simulation and audio model rather than treating these as disconnected effects.

### 4.5 Discovery and spatial transitions

The reference calls for discoveries that are not all collectibles and describes meaningful transitions between café, market, library, station, sanctuary, garden, and tunnel.

Language-world should author the journey between places as carefully as the destinations. Paths, sightlines, thresholds, sound cues, changing vegetation, lighting, elevation, and small events can create anticipation. The learner should not feel that one screen has simply been replaced by another.

## 5. First language-world concepts recovered

The reference archive contains Japanese and French location concepts. They are useful seeds, not complete or approved world bibles.

### 5.1 Japanese-world location seeds

Real-place examples:
- Shibuya Crossing, Tokyo.
- Gion, Kyoto.
- Dotonbori, Osaka.
- Kanazawa.
- Hakata, Fukuoka.

Fictional place examples:
- Komorebi Café Tokyo.
- Whispering Bamboo Garden.
- Lantern Night Market.
- Hikari Craft House.
- Moonlit Yatai Alley.

These suggest a broad range of urban, historic, craft, garden, food, market, and everyday-life experiences. They must be researched and developed as a coherent geography rather than arranged as unrelated themed stops.

### 5.2 French-world location seeds

Real-place examples:
- Montmartre, Paris.
- Vieux Lyon.
- Strasbourg Old Town.
- Nice.

Fictional place examples:
- La Petite Lune Bakery.
- Place des Histoires.
- Étoile Market Quarter.
- Promenade des Rêves.

These suggest urban neighbourhoods, historic places, markets, bakeries, storytelling, architecture, and Mediterranean coastal experiences. They are not yet a complete map or a definitive cultural representation of France.

### 5.3 Important distinction

The reference implementation's location catalogue provides a small set of alternating real and fictional locations. Its physical presentation layer uses broad theme heuristics and generic road/rail infrastructure for many locations. That is not enough to establish genuinely distinct worlds.

Language-world must author geography first and generate infrastructure, visual identity, travel networks, and experiences from that authored geography—not infer an entire world from a few tags.

## 6. World identity standard

Every world must have a dedicated world bible before substantial content production. Each bible must answer:

### Geography
- What is the world/region's overall shape and scale?
- What are its major landforms, water systems, climate zones, and ecological regions?
- Where are the settlements, landmarks, routes, boundaries, and hidden places?
- What can be seen from each major viewpoint?
- How do places connect physically and through transport?

### Culture and everyday life
- What does ordinary life look and sound like?
- What are local social norms, forms of address, hospitality patterns, and public behaviours?
- What food, craft, music, art, work, education, and leisure belong to this place?
- What should be represented with care, and what stereotypes must be avoided?
- Which details require cultural or linguistic review?

### People and society
- Who lives here and why?
- What communities, occupations, relationships, institutions, and local tensions exist?
- What are residents' biographies, goals, routines, and relationships?
- Which people are recurring characters, and which are ambient inhabitants?
- How does the learner's presence matter without making the world revolve around them?

### Nature and ecology
- Which plant and animal species belong here?
- What habitats, seasonal patterns, daily rhythms, migration, and environmental interactions exist?
- Which creatures are background wildlife, companions, helpers, guardians, or story-critical beings?
- How can ecology be discovered through sound, tracks, silhouettes, behaviour, and habitat rather than only direct encounters?

### Stories and activities
- What local histories, mysteries, stories, festivals, games, and adventures belong here?
- Which stories are independent of Cassidy?
- What can learners do without starting a lesson?
- Which experiences are repeatable, evolving, seasonal, or one-time?
- What lasting consequences are appropriate and how are they recorded?

### Language and learning
- Which real communication situations occur naturally in this world?
- What language varieties, registers, scripts, accents, and cultural context are relevant?
- Which capabilities can be practised and honestly assessed?
- How are language quality and cultural accuracy reviewed?

### Sensory identity
- What are the world-specific colour, material, lighting, weather, and sound languages?
- What does the world sound like from its central places and at different times?
- What is its musical identity, and where should music be absent?
- How does the atmosphere change across weather, season, and time?

### Production and technical needs
- What is the world's asset vocabulary and replacement strategy?
- Which spaces are active, streamed, distant, or represented in 2D/2.5D?
- What are its navigation, collision, interaction, accessibility, and performance requirements?
- What can persist and what is simulated?
- What are its quality and cultural-review acceptance gates?

## 7. Canonical universe registry requirements

Language-world needs one authoritative registry for:
- Universe spaces and their type.
- Worlds and their language/locale associations.
- Regions, settlements, locations, and sublocations.
- Real versus fictional place status and source/research provenance.
- Geography, climate, ecology, and transport network references.
- Residents, creatures, factions, and community affiliations.
- Stories, quests, festivals, games, and activity availability.
- Asset packages, world-specific audio profiles, and presentation settings.
- World content versions, dependencies, and migration rules.

Authored world definitions must be separate from learner-specific state such as visited places, discovered clues, unlocked experiences, personal creations, and remembered events.

IDs must be stable semantic identities. Display names, translations, asset filenames, engine nodes, and map coordinates are not primary identity.

## 8. Travel and world connections

Travel is a world experience, not a menu operation or a line drawn between two map points.

The universe must support:
- Authored route networks and travel hubs.
- Different modes for inter-world and intra-world journeys.
- Departure/arrival staging and contextual cinematics.
- Durable travel state that survives pause, interruption, and relaunch.
- Geography and time/distance consistency.
- Destination reveal and a playable handoff into the new world.

The user's established example—travelling from Emerald Valley to a Spanish-language world by train—remains a product requirement to design. The Spanish world itself is not defined in this draft. Planes may serve longer routes; cars and buses may serve local or regional travel where appropriate to the world.

## 9. What must not happen

- Do not make Emerald Valley the container for all languages.
- Do not turn language worlds into recoloured versions of one template.
- Do not use flags, food, costumes, or famous landmarks as the entire cultural identity.
- Do not treat a handful of tourist locations as a complete country or culture.
- Do not make every resident a quest giver or language tutor.
- Do not require Cassidy to explain every place or initiate every activity.
- Do not turn all wildlife into collectibles or enemies.
- Do not use discovery as a synonym for XP or reward drops.
- Do not simulate every world continuously at full fidelity.
- Do not copy the old physical maps, coordinates, scene composition, assets, or renderer as implementation.

## 10. Open universe decisions

1. Which language worlds are in the first release, and which are later expansions?
2. Which languages and locales are supported in each world, including dialect/register treatment?
3. Is the first Spanish-language world the next destination after Emerald Valley, and what is its identity?
4. How much real geography versus fictional geography should each world contain?
5. What is the canonical universe history and why are these worlds connected?
6. Which transport hubs and route network connect the first worlds?
7. Which world-specific creatures and ecological systems are essential to the initial universe?
8. What is the product's target audience and how does that affect content boundaries?
9. What cultural review process and expert contributors are available?
