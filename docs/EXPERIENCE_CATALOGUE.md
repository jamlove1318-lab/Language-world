# Language-world — Experience Catalogue

**Status:** Working specification v0.1; not approved for implementation.  
**Purpose:** Define what people can do in the universe, how experiences are authored, and what makes them worth returning to.  
**Source boundary:** GoPAL-AI is a read-only research archive. This document carries forward ideas only; no legacy code, assets, data structures, or production pipeline are dependencies.

## 1. Foundational principle

Language-world is a place to inhabit and return to, not a menu of lessons disguised as a 3D map. An experience can be worthwhile because it is moving, funny, mysterious, beautiful, useful, surprising, social, creative, relaxing, or intellectually satisfying. It does not need to award points or test vocabulary to justify its existence.

Language learning should be woven naturally into the universe. Sometimes it is the central challenge; sometimes it is embedded in a broader activity; sometimes it is incidental exposure; sometimes it is absent. A learner should be able to explore, enjoy a story, watch a festival, create something, or follow a mystery without every interaction becoming a test.

**The world is not a reward screen. The learner is not a task-completion machine.**

## 2. Experience families

These families are an authoring and discovery taxonomy, not mutually exclusive product silos. A mystery can include exploration, dialogue, puzzles, a game, and a lasting world consequence.

### Exploration and discovery
Roam through authored spaces; discover paths, landmarks, hidden rooms, viewpoints, quiet places, and environmental details. Notice unusual sounds, footprints, weather, lights, objects out of place, or changes to familiar locations. Inspect objects for practical, historical, cultural, or narrative meaning. Return at another time or season and notice what has changed. Discoveries need not all be collectible tokens: some are knowledge, access, context, memories, or simply a beautiful moment.

### Mysteries and investigations
Environmental mysteries can combine physical evidence, testimony, sound, documents, maps, and spatial clues. Include small mysteries solvable in one visit and larger threads unfolding over multiple visits. Possible subjects include missing objects, strange messages, unexplained events, local history, folklore, and the secrets of a place. Distinguish evidence from speculation. Clues may be missed without permanently breaking the story; provide alternate evidence paths and a reviewable clue journal. Reward observation and reasoning, not arbitrary pixel hunting. Allow pause, exit, and return.

### Stories and adventures
Support standalone short stories, multi-part adventures, character stories, world stories, seasonal narratives, historical narratives, folklore-inspired fiction, and wider universe arcs. Stories may be linear, branching, exploratory, episodic, or systemic; do not force every story into a dialogue tree. Choices should have legible meaning and may change knowledge, access, relationships, world state, dialogue, or endings. Not every choice needs a permanent branch. Stories may concern residents, creatures, places, objects, communities, or events. Cassidy is not required to appear. The world must contain stories that remain interesting when the learner is absent and do not exist solely to deliver a lesson. Avoid fake stakes, endless cliffhangers, and artificial waiting timers.

### Games and playful challenges
Create self-contained playable mechanics with clear rules, feedback, a beginning, and an ending. Games can be quick, thoughtful, cooperative, exploratory, rhythmic, strategic, creative, narrative, or movement-based. A game may teach language, but can also develop observation, spatial reasoning, memory, cultural understanding, or creativity—or simply provide delight. Timed play is optional, never the default. Provide relaxed or untimed alternatives where feasible. A game must be enjoyable before rewards are considered. Cosmetic reskins do not count as meaningful variety.

### Language-in-the-world
Include believable everyday interactions: ordering, asking directions, greeting, arranging, clarifying, apologising, negotiating, helping, and responding to unexpected situations. Include listening to announcements, conversations, stories, music and environmental speech; reading signs, menus, letters, maps, notices, books and messages; and speaking, pronunciation, shadowing, writing, typing, composing and interpreting. Every core-learning experience must state its learning purpose and valid assessment method. Do not claim to measure pronunciation or fluency when only a selected answer or transcript is checked. Real-world settings should not become generic arcade hubs.

### Culture and everyday life
Explore food, craft, architecture, public spaces, etiquette, festivals, art, music, regional variation, history, and contemporary everyday life through participation, observation, conversation, making, and stories—not only fact cards. Avoid treating a country or language community as a single stereotype. Distinguish researched real-world information from fictional worldbuilding. Real locations and traditions require cultural and language review.

### Creative experiences
Enable postcards, letters, journals, phrase collections, illustrated stories, comics, maps, recipes, designs, room arrangements, galleries, memory boards, and constructed scenes. Saved work is user-owned and needs a lifecycle: create, preview, edit, reopen, duplicate, delete, export where supported, and manage attached media. Explain whether work is private, shared, or public before publication. Do not silently repurpose creative work as training data or public content. Templates may help, but the user retains meaningful authorship.

### Social and community experiences
Optional shared activities may include cooperative puzzles, celebrations, clubs, message exchanges, collaborative creations, and asynchronous contributions. Single-player remains complete; social participation never gates core progression. Privacy, moderation, reporting, blocking, age-appropriate defaults, and safe user-generated content handling must be designed before public interaction. Never expose location, schedule, personal history, or private archive to other users. Avoid leaderboards and social comparison as the primary motivation.

### World-life and environment
Let learners observe residents following their own routines, relationships, responsibilities, and plans; watch wildlife; help restore a habitat; tend a garden; observe plant growth; visit a café, market, workshop, library, sanctuary, or festival; or simply sit and watch the weather. Time, season, sound, lighting, and local events may change the experience without forcing an activity. Observation without interaction is valid.

### Travel and transitions
A journey can include preparation, a station or terminal, boarding, departure, travel, arrival, and destination discovery. Travel should express geography, distance, anticipation, and destination identity. Cinematic presentation is authored as an experience; persistent travel state and arrival are owned by world/travel systems. Local transport must fit the world's geography and infrastructure. Travel is not merely a loading screen with a vehicle animation.

### Events, reflection, and personal archive
Support festivals, seasonal changes, performances, exhibitions, markets, community projects, and story-linked happenings. Important narrative content must not become permanently missable because a learner did not open the app on one day. A Journey Book or archive may preserve discoveries, stories, creations, learned phrases, cultural notes, and milestones. It is a record, not a mandatory checklist. Memories need provenance: what happened, when, where, how it was recorded, and whether it can be edited or deleted. Never fabricate a shared memory or claim Cassidy remembers an event that did not occur.

## 3. Cassidy's role

Cassidy is the emotional anchor, but not a compulsory presenter, narrator, tutor, quest-giver, or reward dispenser. She may invite, accompany, participate, disagree, discover something independently, or spend time elsewhere. Her own stories and adventures can happen without the learner; their consequences may later be encountered. She should not interrupt every quiet moment with an offer and must never express guilt, loneliness, jealousy, abandonment, or distress because of absence. Her appearance must be narratively justified. Her identity, agency, boundaries, dialogue, autonomy, and performance are governed by the [Cassidy Character Bible](CASSIDY_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md).

## 4. Learning relationship

Every experience declares one of these relationships:

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Core learning | Language is the main objective and explicitly practised or assessed | Understanding a station announcement |
| Embedded learning | Language is used naturally in a broader activity | Asking a craftsperson about a technique |
| Incidental exposure | Meaningful language is encountered without a required test | Hearing residents converse |
| Cultural/contextual | Develops understanding of a practice, place, or context | Preparing for a festival |
| Non-learning | Exists for story, exploration, creativity, atmosphere, or play | Watching a storm cross the valley |

For core learning, specify target language and variety/register, proficiency assumptions, skills, observable learner action, evidence collected, feedback and hint policy, acceptable alternatives, uncertainty, accessibility alternatives, and what progress data is retained. A multiple-choice selection, typed response, ASR transcript, and measured speech characteristics are different evidence types. Do not treat one success percentage as universal mastery.

## 5. Recovered 60 game ideas — research seeds only

The GoPAL-AI archive documents 60 game concepts. Its own catalogue says the ideas are documented while playable implementations, variety selection, world-reactive outcomes, and cross-language reuse remained future work. A separate code catalogue and session engine provide a foundation, not proof that all 60 are complete, balanced, accessible, or fun. The list below is not a launch commitment.

**Discovery/search:** Word Hunt; Object Snap; Sign Spotter; Hidden Phrase; Culture Clue Hunt; Memory Trail; Lost Label; Sound Scout.

**Building/puzzles:** Phrase Builder; Grammar Garden; Sentence Machine; Particle Puzzle; Verb Workshop; Word Forge; Kanji Tile Forge; Translation Tangle; Context Lock; Logic Lanterns.

**Arcade/reflex:** Speed Round; Falling Words; Word Dash; Listening Chase; Quick Reply; Flash Recall; Match Rush; Sound Catcher; Grammar Dodge; Vocabulary Meteor.

**Social/communication:** Politeness Duel; Dialogue Detective; Market Rush; Café Order; Train Counter; Neighbor Favor; Lost Tourist; Message Relay; Emotion Reader; Conversation Repair.

**Listening/audio:** Echo Station; Audio Map; Voice Match; Missing Word Radio; Street Noise; Rhythm Repeat; Announcement Alert; Whisper Trail.

**Story/adventure:** Mystery Room; Story Choice; Detective Case; Treasure Map; Festival Prep; Time Traveler; Dream Theatre; Secret Agent; Escape the Archive; Lantern Mystery.

**Creative/construction:** Sign Designer; Story Composer; Recipe Creator; World Builder.

These ideas should be re-evaluated rather than copied mechanically. A timed word-catching game can be an optional arcade challenge, not a substitute for natural conversation. A fictional grammar garden can be playful; a real cultural location should not become an arbitrary game menu.

## 6. Experience authoring contract

Each experience record must include:

**Identity:** stable ID, content version, title, family, author/editor, world/region/place, real/fictional status, provenance, content owner, review status.

**Player experience:** emotional tone, player fantasy, opening hook, reason to participate, player role, rules, controls, core loop, variations, expected duration, start/pause/resume/exit/fail/retry/complete behaviour, solo/social requirements, optionality.

**Learning and content:** learning relationship, target language and variety, proficiency, skill outcomes, evidence, hints, dialogue/audio/text, localization, cultural review, accessibility and alternative input.

**Narrative and world:** story beats, characters, choices, clue dependencies, endings, durable consequences for residents/places/ecology/objects, return path, and how the learner reviews what happened.

**Production and operations:** required art/animation/audio/voice/UI, semantic asset IDs and fallbacks, target-device performance tier, save/sync, privacy classification, retention, content QA, language QA, cultural QA, safety review, and acceptance evidence.

## 7. Discovery and variety

An experience director may help the learner discover activities, but must not become an opaque engagement optimiser.

- Respect explicit intent: explore, story, practise, create, relax, challenge, or surprise.
- Give an honest reason for a suggestion; make it dismissible.
- Use context (place, available time, history, accessibility settings, learner goals) only when relevant.
- Avoid repetitive mechanics and presentation patterns, but do not hide a favourite just to force novelty.
- Do not infer that success means the learner wants harder challenges.
- Never use sensitive data, emotional vulnerability, or absence patterns to increase pressure.
- “Surprise me” remains bounded by safety, age suitability, accessibility, and preferences.
- Recommendations include non-learning experiences, not only activities that improve a progress metric.

## 8. Difficulty, failure, and pacing

Difficulty should be adjustable and transparent. Prefer scaffolding over punitive failure. Let learners retry, request hints, inspect explanations, or leave and return. Time limits must be optional or justified by the game fantasy. Avoid lives/hearts that interrupt stories or block content. One incorrect answer must not permanently close a narrative path. Adaptive support should improve understanding, not silently lower content quality. Provide calm modes for reflex, audio, and visually demanding activities where feasible. Daily streak requirements, countdown pressure, and expiring access are not default design tools.

## 9. Persistence and continuity

Keep these state classes distinct:

- **Session:** temporary selections, timer, current puzzle step; discard only when communicated.
- **Learner evidence:** responses, attempts, hints, confidence/uncertainty, skill evidence, review state.
- **Narrative:** clues, choices, hypotheses, chapter progress, unlocked scenes, endings.
- **World:** object changes, repaired places, gardens/ecology, event outcomes, resident commitments.
- **Personal archive:** user-created work, memories, journal entries, collected references.
- **Travel:** origin/destination, transport stage, route, arrival handoff.
- **Social:** shared contributions and moderation records, if enabled.

Durable records must be learner/account scoped, versioned, and have an explicit offline/sync policy. Recover sessions after termination or interruption. Completion and rewards must be idempotent. Multi-system outcomes must not depend on a fragile sequence of unrelated local writes. Detailed storage and event contracts belong in the dedicated architecture specification; this document establishes product requirements, not a storage implementation.

## 10. Accessibility, safety, inclusion

Provide captions/transcripts, speaker identification, adjustable text, contrast, audio levels, playback speed, repeat controls, reduced motion, cinematic skip/shorten options, and alternatives to colour-only, rapid-reaction, precise-touch, or sound-only tasks. Provide alternatives for microphone, speech recognition, handwriting, and device-dependent input. Do not require camera, contacts, precise location, or public profile for core experiences. Social features require moderation and age-appropriate safeguards. Avoid stereotypes and clearly distinguish fictional inventions from cultural facts.

## 11. Quality gates

An experience is not ready merely because it opens.

1. **Concept:** Is it distinct, worth the learner's time, and suited to its place/story? Is it more than a checklist or reward task?
2. **Playability:** Are rules clear, feedback fair, controls comfortable, and pause/resume/recovery reliable? Is it enjoyable without rewards?
3. **Learning integrity:** Are outcomes specific and evidence valid for the claimed skill? Are ambiguity, dialect/register, hints, and feedback handled?
4. **World/narrative integrity:** Do consequences persist? Are clues recoverable? Are characters consistent? Is cultural/ecological identity respected?
5. **Production readiness:** Are assets replaceable through semantic contracts? Are performance, memory, audio, loading, localization, accessibility, privacy, and safety reviewed on representative Android hardware?
6. **Live quality:** Can content be versioned/corrected without corrupting learner state? Can user-owned work survive experience retirement?

## 12. Candidate first-release portfolio

Do not ship all 60 research seeds or populate every world at once. A candidate balanced portfolio should demonstrate: one memorable exploration experience; one mystery with recoverable clues; one character-led story and one story independent of Cassidy; one natural language interaction; one listening experience with replay/transcript; one playful game with a distinct mechanic; one creative activity with editable saved work; one world-life moment without a task; and, if a destination world is included, one short travel/arrival experience. Include a small environmental event or visible world consequence.

This is a planning target, not an approved release commitment. Scope depends on completed world bibles, learning design, cinematic specification, production capacity, and target-device evidence.

## 15. Home-world identity and broad learning choice

Emerald Valley is the learner's home, not the singular learning destination or a course to complete. It should support everyday conversations with Cassidy and residents, local life, exploration, casual encounters, stories, games, puzzles, optional learning, and a familiar place to return to.

Learning is available across the wider universe and through many formats. The catalogue should offer a meaningful mix of conversations, guided scenarios, listening, reading, vocabulary and grammar in context, speaking and writing practice, quizzes, puzzles, mini-games, interactive stories, mysteries, cultural activities, and focused learning spaces. The learner can choose among these formats rather than being forced through one linear curriculum route.

Locations provide distinct contexts rather than all becoming classrooms. A market, café, library, workshop, station, port, museum, garden, trail, theatre, or dedicated challenge venue may each support different experiences. Their exact launch inclusion is a scope decision.

The learner's home can also develop. Buildings and scenarios may be upgraded through the points progression system, with visible improvements and new optional variants. The Experience Catalogue defines the activities and scenario content; the World Objects and Spaces specification defines building/upgrade state and spatial contracts; the points domain owns earn/spend transactions. These responsibilities must remain separate.

This is a product requirement and working direction. The first-release portfolio, language scope, exact location list, point economy, and upgrade trees remain open.
